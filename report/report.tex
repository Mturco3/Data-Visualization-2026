\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Over the past decade, platforms like Airbnb have transformed how people travel and, crucially,
how cities allocate their housing stock. In Milan, Italy's economic capital and one of Europe's
most visited cities, the number of active short-term rental listings has grown dramatically
since 2018, raising a pressing question for residents and policymakers alike:

\begin{quote}\itshape
  How has the rise of short-term rentals reshaped Milan's urban housing landscape between 2018
  and 2024, and is there a measurable spatial and temporal correlation with the long-term
  rental market?
\end{quote}

This project uses Milan as a case study of a broader European phenomenon. We combine two public
datasets, Airbnb listing data and OMI official rent estimates from the Italian Revenue Agency,
to map where short-term rental pressure is highest and whether those same areas show rising
long-term rents.

\smallskip
\noindent\textbf{Honest scope.}
We measure \emph{market pressure} (correlation between Airbnb growth and rent levels), not
direct displacement. Measuring displacement would require eviction or registry data we do not
have. The distinction between pressure and displacement is itself an interpretive choice, and
we make it explicit in Section~\ref{sec:hats}.

\noindent\textbf{Target audience.}
Urban planners, students, local residents, and policy observers who need clear spatial and
temporal evidence rather than purely technical metrics.

\section{Data Sources}
\label{sec:data}

\subsection{Inside Airbnb - Milan}

Listing-level snapshot from Inside Airbnb (2025), with the embedded review history used as a
temporal proxy to reconstruct activity from 2018 to 2024.
Source: \href{https://insideairbnb.com/get-the-data/}{insideairbnb.com/get-the-data}.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \textbf{Reviews dataset:} Review-level records (listing\_id and review date) are used
        to estimate yearly Airbnb activity when full historical availability is not available.
  \item \textbf{Granularity:} Individual listing, aggregated by neighbourhood and OMI zone.
  \item \textbf{Key fields:} host\_id, room\_type, price, reviews\_per\_month,
        calculated\_host\_listings\_count, license (CIN), listing\_id, review date.
  \item \textbf{Limitations:} Scraped data; availability calendars are unreliable; no direct
        revenue figures; the review-based activity proxy underestimates listings with few reviews.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{OMI - Agenzia delle Entrate}

Official long-term rent estimates published by the Italian Revenue Agency, by OMI zone and
semester (2018 H1 to 2024 H2).
Source: \href{https://dati.comune.milano.it/dataset?tags=OMI}{dati.comune.milano.it - OMI}.

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item \textbf{Granularity:} OMI zone, mapped to Airbnb neighbourhoods via a custom crosswalk.
  \item \textbf{Key fields:} zona\_omi, typology, rent\_min, rent\_max, semester.
  \item \textbf{Limitations:} OMI publishes estimated ranges, not actual transaction prices;
        OMI zone boundaries do not align with Airbnb neighbourhoods; some zones were merged or
        redrawn during the period.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Custom Neighbourhood-Zone Crosswalk}

OMI zones and Airbnb neighbourhoods use different boundary systems. We built a curated crosswalk
that links each Airbnb neighbourhood to one or more OMI zones, which lets us attach official rent
context to every listing in the snapshot.

\section{Exploratory Analysis and Key Visualisations}
\label{sec:eda}

\subsection{Overview: Current Offer and Neighbourhood Concentration}

The current listing stock is dominated by \emph{entire homes}, which account for approximately
87.9\,\% of the snapshot. Five neighbourhoods (Buenos Aires-Venezia, Duomo, Sarpi,
Centrale, and Loreto) concentrate roughly 28\,\% of all listings, indicating strong
spatial clustering in central and tourist-attractive areas.

\subsection{Minimum-Stay Requirements}

An analysis of the \texttt{minimum\_nights} field reveals how hosts position their listings
on the market. Most listings fall in the short-stay category (1 to 6 nights), confirming a
predominantly tourist-oriented supply. A non-trivial share of listings requires 30 or more
nights, signalling segments that operate closer to the medium-term rental market.

\subsection{Temporal Dynamics: 2018 to 2024}

Review activity is used as a proxy for listing activity over time. Key observations:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.4em]
  \item Review activity fell by approximately 67.5\,\% from 2019 to 2020, reflecting the
        impact of the COVID-19 pandemic on tourism.
  \item Recovery was rapid and strong. By 2024, the yearly review count was approximately
        329\,\% above the 2019 level.
  \item Official OMI rents moved more sluggishly: they declined modestly in 2020 to 2021 and
        then recovered, but without matching the dramatic rebound in Airbnb activity.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{3D Isometric Map: ``The Invasion''}

A Three.js isometric visualisation encodes two dimensions simultaneously: palace height
represents the OMI midpoint rent (\euro{}/m\textsuperscript{2}/month) for each neighbourhood,
while floating balloons above each palace represent review-inferred active listings (one balloon
per 80 reviewed listings). Selecting any year from 2018 to 2024 animates the change in both
dimensions, making spatial inequality and temporal growth immediately visible.

The visualisation is accessible on the project website:
\href{https://mturco3.github.io/Data-Visualization-2026/}{mturco3.github.io/Data-Visualization-2026}.

\section{White Hat / Black Hat}
\label{sec:hats}

The same dataset, two radically different framings. Both are technically accurate. Only one
is honest.

\subsection{White Hat: Transparent and Constructive View}

The white hat figure uses the full overlapping period from 2018 to 2024 and indexes both
series to the same baseline (2018~=~100). The reader can see that Airbnb review activity
rebounds much faster than official rents, but the figure does not imply direct causation. The
design choices (shared baseline, full time window, single axis, and neutral annotation)
give the reader a fair chance to judge how similar or dissimilar the trajectories really are.

\subsection{Black Hat: Misleading Framing}

The black hat figure uses the same underlying citywide series but applies several manipulative
design decisions:

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em]
  \item Only the post-2021 rebound is shown, erasing the pandemic break and making the
        recovery look like a seamless acceleration.
  \item Dual vertical axes with different scales amplify the visual alignment between two
        variables that live on completely different magnitudes.
  \item The rent axis is truncated to exaggerate variation.
  \item The title turns a visual coincidence into a causal slogan:
        \emph{``Airbnb explodes and Milan rents follow immediately''}.
\end{enumerate}

The lesson is useful beyond this project: ethical problems in visualisation often come from
emphasis and framing rather than fabricated data. Every data point can be real, yet the
reader is still pushed toward a stronger conclusion than the evidence supports.

\section{Key Findings}
\label{sec:findings}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=1.6em]
  \item \textbf{Concentration is real but not citywide uniform.}
        The five largest neighbourhoods account for about 28\,\% of all listings, with
        Buenos Aires-Venezia and Duomo standing out most clearly.

  \item \textbf{Current Airbnb presence aligns with expensive rent contexts.}
        The correlation between listing stock and linked 2024 OMI midpoint rent is positive
        ($r \approx 0.62$). This is a cross-sectional association, not causal proof.

  \item \textbf{COVID was a sharp interruption, not a reset.}
        Review activity fell by about 67.5\,\% from 2019 to 2020, then rebounded strongly.
        By 2024 the yearly review count was roughly 329\,\% above the 2019 level.

  \item \textbf{The stock is heavily commercialised.}
        Entire homes represent about 87.9\,\% of the snapshot, and hosts with at least five
        listings account for about 45.1\,\%.

  \item \textbf{Pressure does not automatically imply displacement.}
        The data show a meaningful overlap between Airbnb concentration and high rent context,
        but not direct evidence of displacement. That would require eviction or registry data.
\end{enumerate}
