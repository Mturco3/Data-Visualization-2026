Exploring the spatial and temporal correlation between short-term rentals and long-term rent prices in Milan (2018–2024)

↓

Introduction

Urban planners & citizens

Over the past decade, platforms like Airbnb have transformed how people travel and, crucially, how cities allocate their housing stock. In Milan — Italy's economic capital and one of Europe's most visited cities — the number of active short-term rental listings has grown dramatically since 2018, raising a pressing question for residents and policymakers alike:

How has the rise of short-term rentals reshaped Milan's urban housing landscape between 2018 and 2024, and is there a measurable spatial and temporal correlation with the long-term rental market?

This project uses Milan as a case study of a broader European phenomenon. We combine two public datasets: Airbnb listing data and OMI official rent estimates from the Italian Revenue Agency; to map where short-term rental pressure is highest and whether those same areas show rising long-term rents.

Honest scope: We measure market pressure (correlation between Airbnb growth and rent levels), not direct displacement. Measuring displacement would require eviction or registry data we do not have. The distinction between pressure and displacement is itself an interpretive choice, and we make it explicit in the White / Black Hat section below.

Data Sources

A

Inside Airbnb — Milan

  • What: Listing-level snapshot from Inside Airbnb (2025), with the embedded review history used as a temporal proxy to reconstruct activity from 2018 to 2024.
  • Reviews dataset: Review-level records from Inside Airbnb, especially listing_id and review date, are used to estimate yearly Airbnb activity when full historical availability is not available.
  • Granularity: Individual listing → aggregated by neighbourhood and OMI zone.
  • Key fields: host_id, room_type, price, reviews_per_month, calculated_host_listings_count, license (CIN), listing_id, review date.
  • Limitations: Scraped data; availability calendars are unreliable; no direct revenue figures; the review-based activity proxy underestimates listings with few reviews.
O

OMI — Agenzia delle Entrate

  • What: Official long-term rent estimates published by the Italian Revenue Agency, by OMI zone and semester (2018 H1, 2024 H2).
  • Granularity: OMI zone → mapped to Airbnb neighbourhoods via our custom crosswalk.
  • Key fields: zona_omi, typology, rent_min, rent_max, semester.
  • Limitations: OMI publishes estimated ranges, not actual transaction prices; OMI zone boundaries do not align with Airbnb neighbourhoods (hence the crosswalk); some zones were merged or redrawn during the period.
Custom mapping: OMI zones and Airbnb neighbourhoods use different boundary systems. We built a curated crosswalk that links each Airbnb neighbourhood to one or more OMI zones, which lets us attach official rent context to every listing in the snapshot.

Before the dashboard

Airbnb Pressure And Short-Term Rentals

In Italian public debate the issue is often called affitti brevi, or short-term rentals: homes that function less like ordinary housing and more like flexible tourist accommodation. The dashboard starts from that debate, but the first question is simpler: is platform activity actually rising, and do listing rules look compatible with very short stays?

What does the data say?

Reviews give the clearest temporal signal: after the COVID interruption, activity rises very fast. Minimum-stay rules add the operational clue: most listings allow stays under one week, which is exactly the rental logic residents and policymakers worry about.

Interactive Dashboard

Citywide overview, zone selection, and market context in one frame

The dashboard reads Milan from the top down: first the citywide Airbnb and rent-market indicators, then the neighbourhood map, then the yearly review and OMI rent trajectories. The final panels compare residential typologies and test whether higher Airbnb prices tend to sit in higher-rent zones.

Read The Map First

Start with the Milan map: selecting a zone turns the dashboard from a citywide overview into a local comparison of listings, reviews, hosts, Airbnb prices, and OMI rent levels.

Separate Tempo From Level

Reviews move like a platform signal, with a COVID break and a fast rebound. OMI rents move more slowly, so the dashboard keeps growth rates and rent levels visually distinct.

Use Correlation Carefully

The scatter view suggests alignment between expensive Airbnb offers and expensive rent environments, but the spread across zones is the point: pressure is spatially uneven.

The Invasion — 3D Isometric Map of Milan

JavaScript / Three.js

Each terracotta palace represents a neighbourhood: its height encodes the official OMI midpoint rent (€/m²/month). The coral balloons floating above each palace represent yearly Airbnb review volume. The exact visual rule is height = max(rent_mid × 0.55, 0.06) and balloons = min(ceil(review_count / 80), 12). Select a year from 2018–2024 and watch the city rise. Scroll to zoom.

White Hat / Black Hat

The same dataset, two radically different framings. Both are technically accurate. Only one is honest.

White Hat

Transparent & Constructive View

Uses the full overlapping period from 2018 to 2024 and indexes both series to the same baseline. The reader can see that Airbnb review activity rebounds much faster than official rents, but the figure does not imply direct causation.

Black Hat

Misleading Framing

Uses the same underlying citywide series but keeps only the post-2021 rebound, then truncates the rent axis and relies on dual scales. The numbers remain real, but the framing pushes the viewer toward a much stronger conclusion than the data can support.

Key Findings

  1. Concentration is real but not citywide uniform: The five largest neighbourhoods account for about 28 % of all listings, with Buenos Aires – Venezia and Duomo standing out most clearly.
  2. Current Airbnb presence aligns with expensive rent contexts: The correlation between listing stock and linked 2024 OMI midpoint rent is positive (r ≈ 0.62). This is a cross-sectional association, not causal proof.
  3. COVID was a sharp interruption, not a reset: Review activity fell by about 67.5 % from 2019 to 2020, then rebounded strongly. By 2024 the yearly review count was roughly 329 % above the 2019 level.
  4. The stock is heavily commercialized: Entire homes represent about 87.9 % of the snapshot, and hosts with at least five listings account for about 45.1 %.
  5. Pressure does not automatically imply displacement: The data show a meaningful overlap between Airbnb concentration and high rent context, but not direct evidence of displacement. That would require eviction or registry data.
