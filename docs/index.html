Introduction

Urban planners & citizens

Over the past decade, platforms like Airbnb have transformed how people travel and, crucially, how cities allocate their housing stock. In Milan — Italy's economic capital and one of Europe's most visited cities — the number of active short-term rental listings has grown dramatically since 2018, raising a pressing question for residents and policymakers alike:

How has the rise of short-term rentals reshaped Milan's urban housing landscape between 2018 and 2024, and is there a measurable spatial and temporal correlation with the long-term rental market?

This project uses Milan as a case study of a broader European phenomenon. We combine two public datasets — Airbnb listing data and OMI official rent estimates from the Italian Revenue Agency — to map where short-term rental pressure is highest and whether those same areas show rising long-term rents.

Honest scope: We measure market pressure (correlation between Airbnb growth and rent levels), not direct displacement. Displacement would require eviction or registry data we do not have. The distinction is a key theme in the White / Black Hat section below.

Data Sources

A

Inside Airbnb — Milan

  • What: Listing-level snapshot downloaded in 2025 plus review history used for the 2018–2024 overlap
  • Granularity: Individual listing → aggregated by neighbourhood
  • Key fields: host_id, room_type, price, reviews_per_month, license (CIN)
  • Limitations: Scraped data; availability calendars can be unreliable; no direct revenue figures
O

OMI — Agenzia delle Entrate

  • What: Official rent-range estimates per OMI zone, every semester
  • Granularity: OMI zone → mapped to Airbnb neighbourhoods by us
  • Key fields: zona_omi, typology, rent_min, rent_max, semester
  • Limitations: Ranges (not transactions); zone boundaries differ from neighbourhood boundaries; some zones merged over time
Custom mapping: OMI zones and Airbnb neighbourhoods use different boundary systems. We therefore use a curated crosswalk table that links each Airbnb neighbourhood name to an OMI zone before attaching official rent context to the listing snapshot.

Creative #2

Pressure Grid — Hexagonal Comparison View

Each hexagonal cell represents one neighbourhood in a structured comparison grid rather than a literal geographic map. Colour encodes the number of listings in the current Airbnb snapshot, while cell size encodes the linked 2024 OMI midpoint rent. Neighbourhoods that are both large and dark stand out because they combine dense short-term rental presence with an expensive formal rent context.

White Hat / Black Hat

The same dataset, two radically different framings. Both are technically accurate. Only one is honest.

White Hat

Transparent & Constructive View

Uses the full overlapping period from 2018 to 2024 and indexes both series to the same baseline. The reader can see that Airbnb review activity rebounds much faster than official rents, but the figure does not imply direct causation.

Black Hat

Misleading Framing

Uses the same underlying citywide series but keeps only the post-2021 rebound, then truncates the rent axis and relies on dual scales. The numbers remain real, but the framing pushes the viewer toward a much stronger conclusion than the data can support.

Key Findings

  1. Concentration is real but not citywide uniform: The five largest neighbourhoods account for about 28 % of all listings, with Buenos Aires – Venezia and Duomo standing out most clearly.
  2. Current Airbnb presence aligns with expensive rent contexts: The correlation between listing stock and linked 2024 OMI midpoint rent is positive (r ≈ 0.62). This is a cross-sectional association, not causal proof.
  3. COVID was a sharp interruption, not a reset: Review activity fell by about 67.5 % from 2019 to 2020, then rebounded strongly. By 2024 the yearly review count was roughly 329 % above the 2019 level.
  4. The stock is heavily commercialized: Entire homes represent about 87.9 % of the snapshot, and hosts with at least five listings account for about 45.1 %.
  5. Pressure does not automatically imply displacement: The data show a meaningful overlap between Airbnb concentration and high rent context, but not direct evidence of displacement. That would require eviction or registry data.
